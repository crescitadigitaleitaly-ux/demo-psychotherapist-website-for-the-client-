Understanding attachment in adult relationships

Why the need for closeness does not disappear when we grow up, and what it can look like when that need feels at risk.

Most of us know the feeling: a partner seems distant, a message goes unanswered, and something inside tightens. It can seem out of proportion to what actually happened. Attachment theory offers one way of understanding why these moments can feel so significant.

Where the idea comes from

Attachment theory grew out of work in the mid-twentieth century observing how young children respond when separated from, and reunited with, the people who care for them. Children seek closeness when they are frightened or upset, and they settle more easily when someone reliable responds. Researchers later began to ask whether adults show similar patterns in their closest relationships, particularly with romantic partners.

The broad finding is reassuring in its ordinariness: adults also turn to trusted people for comfort and a sense of security. Needing others is not a sign of weakness. It is part of how human beings are built.

When connection feels uncertain

When we feel securely connected, it is easier to be curious, to tolerate disagreement and to ask for what we need. When connection feels uncertain, many people fall back on one of two broad strategies.

  • Reaching harder. Seeking reassurance, pressing for a response, or becoming critical in an attempt to be noticed.
  • Pulling back. Going quiet, focusing on tasks, or minimising feelings to keep things calm and avoid getting hurt.

Both make sense as forms of self-protection. Each may have been learned early, in relationships where one strategy worked better than the other. The difficulty is that, in adult relationships, these strategies can set each other off.

Our protective moves often make perfect sense on the inside, while looking quite different from the outside.

Styles, not labels

You may have come across terms such as “anxious” or “avoidant” attachment. They can be useful shorthand, but they are best treated as descriptions of tendencies rather than fixed identities. Most people show different patterns with different people, and at different times in their lives. Relationships, experiences and therapy can all shift how secure we feel.

Why understanding helps

Recognising your own way of responding when connection feels shaky can bring a little more choice. Instead of only reacting, you might notice the feeling underneath, perhaps fear of being left, or worry about not being good enough, and find another way to express it.

For couples, understanding each other’s patterns can soften blame. A partner who withdraws may be trying to prevent things getting worse, not showing indifference. A partner who pursues may be signalling how much the relationship matters, not trying to control.

A few questions for reflection

  • When I feel disconnected from someone close to me, what do I tend to do?
  • What am I hoping the other person will understand in those moments?
  • Where might I have learned this way of protecting myself?

These questions do not need tidy answers. They are simply a starting point, and they can be explored further in individual or couples therapy, including approaches informed by Emotionally Focused Therapy.
