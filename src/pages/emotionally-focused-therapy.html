Emotionally Focused Therapy · EFT

Understanding the emotions that connect us

Emotionally Focused Therapy is a structured, attachment-based approach to therapy. It looks closely at emotions, the needs beneath them, and the patterns people fall into when connection feels at risk.

What EFT means

Emotionally Focused Therapy, usually shortened to EFT, was developed in the 1980s by psychologists Sue Johnson and Les Greenberg, first as a way of working with couples. It has since been adapted for individuals and for families.

The name describes its focus. Emotions are treated as a central guide in therapy: they tell us what matters, what we fear and what we need from the people closest to us. EFT draws mainly on attachment theory and on research into how emotions work.

Not to be confused with Emotional Freedom Techniques. “EFT” is sometimes used for an unrelated tapping method. On this site, EFT always means Emotionally Focused Therapy, a talking therapy.

Attachment and emotional connection

Attachment theory began with the study of how infants seek closeness and comfort from caregivers. EFT applies the same idea to adult relationships: the need to feel that someone is reachable and responsive does not disappear when we grow up.

When that sense of connection feels uncertain, people respond in understandable ways. Some reach harder; others protect themselves by pulling back. These reactions are seen as attempts to cope, not as character flaws.

Underneath many arguments is a simpler question: can I count on you?

Recurring interaction patterns

A key idea in EFT is the negative cycle: a repeating sequence in which each person’s protective response triggers the other’s. Over time, the cycle itself can come to feel like the enemy. Naming it together, and seeing each person’s part with compassion, is often an early turning point.

The work then attends to the feelings that drive the cycle, which are frequently softer than they first appear: hurt, fear of rejection, loneliness or a sense of failing the other person.

EFT with couples

How EFT may inform couples work

In the EFT literature, couples work is commonly described in three broad stages. In practice the process is flexible, and each couple moves at its own pace.

  1. Stage one

    Calming the cycle

    Partners identify the negative pattern they get caught in and the emotions underneath each person’s position, so conflict becomes less escalated and less blaming.

  2. Stage two

    Reshaping the bond

    With more safety in the room, each partner is supported to share deeper feelings and needs, and to respond to the other in new, more connecting ways.

  3. Stage three

    Consolidating change

    The couple practises these new patterns in everyday issues and builds a shared understanding of how they found their way back to each other.

Summary prepared from published descriptions of the EFT model. Sources are listed in the project’s content notes. EFT is also used with individuals (EFIT) and families (EFFT).

Considering EFT

What you might discuss with a therapist

If you are wondering whether an EFT-informed approach suits you, these are reasonable questions to raise in a first conversation.

  • What training in EFT have you completed, and how do you use it in your work?
  • How do you work with couples where partners want different things?
  • Do you meet with each partner individually as part of assessment?
  • How do you approach safety, and when might couples therapy not be recommended?
  • How will we know whether therapy is helping?

Questions

Questions about EFT

Is EFT only for couples?

No. EFT began as a couples therapy, and there are now versions for individuals (often called EFIT) and families (EFFT). The same attachment-based ideas inform each.

Is EFT the same as tapping or Emotional Freedom Techniques?

No. Emotional Freedom Techniques is an unrelated method that shares the same initials. Emotionally Focused Therapy is a talking therapy grounded in attachment theory.

Does EFT mean we have to talk about childhood?

Not necessarily. The focus is usually on what is happening between you now. Past experiences may come up where they help explain present feelings and reactions.

Is there evidence for EFT?

EFT for couples has been studied in a body of published research and is described by its training organisations as evidence-based. As with any therapy, results differ between people, and no particular outcome can be promised.

How long does EFT take?

Published descriptions often refer to a relatively short-term structured process for couples, but the actual length varies with each couple’s circumstances and goals. It is best discussed directly with the therapist.
